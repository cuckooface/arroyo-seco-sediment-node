# Final review: questions and answers

Drafted from THESIS.md, the drawing labels (DETAIL_SECTION, print set captions) and the midterm deck. Each answer has a line to say out loud, the reasoning behind it, what to show, and where a critic can push. Anything marked **DECIDE** is a design claim Brandon has to confirm or change; it is not yet in his drawings.

---

## 1. Why the unique shapes of the floor plates?

**Say:** There is no typical floor because there is no typical task. Each plate is cut to one step of the loop: receiving, sorting, mixing, growing. And every plate hangs off a single mast, so the plates are shaped and staggered to balance around it.

- **Program sets the outline.** The hopper floor is shaped around the V-chute, the hoist floors leave a void for the bucket line, and the nursery floor opens to light. Each plate is sized for the crew and the equipment that one step needs.
- **One mast sets the balance.** The sliver stands on one footing (P04), so the plates step out on alternating sides of the mast to keep the load centred.
- **The strap sets the edge.** The Dyneema strap wraps the floor sandwich and can't turn a sharp corner without losing strength, so every edge is a bullnose (R = 7½", bent ply band, plies CNC'd to the radius). The rounded plan comes from the lashing, not from style.
- **The site sets the mass.** In plan, the masses fit the banks and reach over the channel between the bridge piers (P06), so the river stays open and the work faces the path.

**Show:** the sliver section with each plate labelled by its loop step, and the detail's bullnose band next to it.

**Push-back:** "One-off shapes are expensive and contradict the network." **Answer:** what repeats from site to site is the assembly and the section (mast, sandwich, strap, veil), not the outline. The outline adapts to each reservoir's bank, the same way the stables adapt here. CLT is CNC-cut either way, so a unique outline costs file time, not material.

## 2. Why do the floors and the envelope peel?

**Say:** The peel is where the building shows its work. The envelope opens where the public should see the work and closes where the crew needs privacy. The floors peel apart so you can read their layers from the river, and so the crew can reach the straps.

- **Envelope = controlled transparency.** The shingle veil (6 mm polycarbonate on ash ribs) is clear where work should be seen and frosted where it shouldn't, as in the housing. Peeling it back exposes a floor or a task to the path and turns the crew into silhouettes (P03).
- **Floors = the section made public.** Where a plate peels past the veil, you see its edge: CLT, cork, earth tile, strap. That edge is the processed sediment, so the product is on display at every floor line.
- **Peel = access.** Wherever a plate pulls away from the one above, there is room for the crew to stand and retension. The print set already says it: "every part stays within the crew's reach to retension." The peel is what makes that true.

**Show:** the render (P03 / `prez_render.jpg`, still unused in the deck) with the peels called out as *seen / private / access*.

**Push-back:** "Peeling is a formal gesture looking for a reason." **Answer:** every peel has to be one of three things: a view, a privacy edge, or an access zone. **DECIDE:** check each peel in the model against those three and cut any that are none of them.

## 3. How much maintenance is usually lost?

**Say:** At Devil's Gate, fifteen years of it. Sediment removal stopped in 1994 and didn't resume until after the 2009 Station Fire, which sent more than 1.3 million cubic yards into the reservoir. That's about three Rose Bowls of sediment, upstream of the Rose Bowl.

| Fact | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Devil's Gate: years with no removal | 1994 to 2009 (15 years) | THESIS.md; LA County history page |
| Sediment added after the Station Fire | 1.3M+ cubic yards | LA County, Devil's Gate history |
| Removed in the 2019–2021 restoration | about 1.3M cubic yards; restores roughly 3M cubic yards of flood capacity | LA County project page |
| County system | 14 reservoirs, 162 debris basins | Sediment Management Strategic Plan 2012–2032 |
| Sediment expected, 2012–2032 | 67.5M cubic yards (about 170 Rose Bowls) | same plan |
| Space left at County placement sites (2011) | about 48M cubic yards, a shortfall of ~20M | same plan |
| Debris basin cleanout trigger | 25% full; only 5% full in a burned watershed | County sediment FAQ |
| Eaton Fire, one storm | about 480,000 cubic yards into 14 surveyed basins | Zenodo study, 2025 |
| Eaton Wash reservoir emergency dig-out | up to 500,000 cubic yards, through May 2025 | LA County fact sheet |
| U.S. stormwater funding gap | $8B a year; 70%+ of communities have no dedicated funding | ASCE 2021 Report Card |

The Rose Bowl comparison is the County's own: the stadium holds about 400,000 cubic yards.

**The point to land:** the County is running out of places to put the sediment (67.5M expected against 48M of space) while the burned slopes need soil. That is move 2 of the thesis, stated in the County's own numbers.

**Push-back:** "Was it really neglect, or permitting and litigation?" Be careful here. The gap after 2009 was stretched by environmental review and lawsuits over the removal project. That actually helps the argument: work nobody watches has no constituency, until it has opponents. Say it that way rather than claiming simple neglect.

## 4. Why are people working the site?

**Say:** Because maintenance is labor, and visibility means nothing if nobody is there to be seen. A machine yard behind a fence is exactly the invisible maintenance this project argues against.

- **The work is hand work.** Testing, sorting ash and debris out, composting, mixing earth, pressing tiles, raising plants and retensioning straps are skilled and continuous. This isn't one pass with a loader.
- **Paid, skilled, permanent, housed.** The crew comes from people in recovery, in reentry, or displaced by the Eaton Fire, and it lives on site in Mass 2.
- **Care at two scales.** A recovering hillside and a recovering person need the same things: time, material and steady attention.
- **People make the constituency.** Ukeles shook every sanitation worker's hand. The public walk passes the crew every day, and that's what makes the work hard to defund.

**Push-back (spectacle):** "Are you putting workers on display?" **Answer:** the veil shows the work, not the workers. Silhouettes, frosted where privacy matters, and the housing is never on view.

## 5. "Temporary healing nodes": why should it be here only temporarily?

**Say:** It shouldn't. The building is permanent, because the sediment never stops coming. What's temporary is everything that passes through it: the sediment leaves as soil, the plants leave for the slopes, and the crew members leave healed, with a trade. The node is a permanent place for temporary healing.

- The thesis rests on permanence: a building "can't be quietly cancelled next fiscal year." Temporary is the failure mode it is answering: emergency dig-outs after Station and after Eaton, then nothing until the next fire.
- **DECIDE:** if "temporary healing node" was a critic's phrase, turn it around as above. If it is your own phrase, rename it ("a permanent node for temporary healing") so it doesn't undercut move 3.

## 6. Why do the tectonics sag?

**Say:** Because the building is a maintenance object. The Dyneema creeps, the earth layer sags, and the crew retensions it in public. The sag is the gauge: you can read from the river path when the building needs care.

- **Creep is real.** Dyneema (UHMWPE) slowly stretches under sustained load. Most designs hide that; this one uses it as a clock.
- **What sags, and what doesn't.** The CLT plate carries the floor. What hangs beneath it, the cork and earth-tile sandwich, is what drops. A hidden steel stop chain is the fail-safe, so the sag never becomes a safety issue.
- **Stewart Brand, *How Buildings Learn*:** buildings live through upkeep. Here the upkeep is designed in, not deferred.

**Show:** two states of one plate side by side, freshly retensioned and sagged to the mark, with the crew at the pad-eye.

**DECIDE / known gap:** the detail does not yet show where the crew retensions the strap (tensioner, pad-eye access, the sag mark). That is the drawing this whole answer depends on. Add it before the final review.

## 7. Why cork?

**Say:** Cork is the one material that compresses and springs back. It keeps the strap tight while the strap creeps. And it's harvested from living trees every nine years or so, without cutting them down: the material is itself a maintenance cycle.

- **The spring.** The detail says it: "expanded cork bead, sandwiched, slightly compressed." The bead is preloaded, so as the Dyneema stretches the cork pushes back, which slows the sag and evens it out.
- **Damping.** The workshop floors take tamping, shovels and wheelbarrows. Cork stops that noise and vibration from reaching the floor below, including the housing.
- **Thermal and moisture.** It insulates, resists rot by the river, and needs no binder (expanded cork is bound by its own resin when steam-baked).
- **Reversible.** Like the earth, it can come apart and go back into a cycle.

## 8. Why cable (the strap)?

**Say:** Because a strap can be tightened by hand. Dyneema is strong enough to hang the earth sandwich, light enough for one person to handle, it doesn't rust beside a river, and it creeps, which turns the structure into a schedule of care.

- **Strength for its weight.** It's among the strongest fibres by weight, and it floats.
- **No corrosion.** Steel cable over a wet channel needs coatings and replacement; Dyneema doesn't rust.
- **Hand maintenance.** It's retensioned at a pad-eye with simple tools, no crane and no welding. That is the right scale for a crew.
- **Honest wear.** Sunlight slowly weakens it, so straps get replaced on a cycle, which is another visible task.
- The hoist lines in the sliver section (buckets on pulleys) follow the same logic: rope-and-pulley work the public can understand at a glance.

## 9. Why that fastening?

**Say:** The fastening is sorted by how often a part needs care. What stays forever is concealed. What the crew maintains is exposed, reachable and reversible by hand.

| Interval | Part (from the detail) | Fastener | Visible? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Permanent | Outrigger arm to edge band | ⅜" knife plate + ¾" dowels | Concealed |
| Permanent | Wall to floor | ¼" steel U-channel shoe, through-bolted, on neoprene pad | Concealed |
| Rarely | Tile carried under the plate | Steel T-hanger lagged to CLT; steel edge bracket | Partly |
| Seasonal | Shingle veil | Stainless J-hook clip: one shingle swaps out without disturbing the rest | Exposed |
| Seasonal | Ash rib to arm | Wedged through-tenon: knock the wedge to tighten or release | Exposed |
| Continuous | Floor sandwich | Dyneema strap at a stainless pad-eye | Exposed |

**Push-back:** "Why mix timber joinery and steel?" **Answer:** wood-to-wood joints that move with moisture (tenons, wedges) are the ones the crew re-seats by hand. Steel goes where the loads concentrate and nothing should ever be adjusted.

## 10. Why rammed earth, and how is it made?

**Say:** Because it's the sediment itself. Debris flow is already a mix of rock, sand, silt and clay; ramming it into walls turns waste into structure with almost nothing added. Every lift in the wall records a storm.

**How it's made from Arroyo sediment:**
1. **Test.** Every load goes to the testing lab first, for ash, lead, asbestos and other contaminants. Anything that fails leaves the cycle. (This is why testing is the program's core.)
2. **Screen.** Boulders and cobbles come out for gabions and channel work; organics go to compost; the fine fraction moves on.
3. **Blend.** Adjust the mix to roughly 50–70% sand and gravel, 15–30% silt and 10–20% clay, adding clay or sand from other loads as needed. The site lab checks each batch.
4. **Moisten.** Bring it to its optimum moisture, around 8–12%. Field check: a squeezed ball holds its shape, and dropped from waist height it shatters rather than splats.
5. **Ram.** Place 6–8" loose lifts in reusable formwork and compact each one to about half its height with pneumatic rammers. Repeat to full height.
6. **Strip and cure.** Forms come off right away and the wall cures over weeks. A roof overhang and a raised base keep water off.

**Stabilize or not? DECIDE.** Adding 5–10% lime or cement makes it stronger and more water-resistant, but then it can never return to soil. Unstabilized walls under roof stay reversible and on-thesis, and the crew patches their surface as part of the maintenance cycle.

**The tile question (known issue).** The detail labels the hung tile "FIRED CLAY TILE", but slide 07 and the thesis say "sediment earth tile." One way to reconcile them: tile **pressed from the node's own clay fraction and low-fired**. An unfired earth tile hanging upside down under a floor near a river isn't durable, so firing is justified. Relabel the detail either way so the drawing and the thesis agree.

**DECIDE:** the current drawings label the walls CLT and ply. If rammed earth is in the project, show and label it (the base, the mass walls, the stable walls?). If it isn't, decide whether the answer is "the earth is in the floor tiles" instead.
